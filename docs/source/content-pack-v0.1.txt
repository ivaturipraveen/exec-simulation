1. Assumptions log
The requirements doc left twelve decisions open and all numeric values provisional. This pack resolves each with a stated assumption so that the content can be played and the engine can be built. Change any row and the dependent content changes with it.
ID
Decision
Assumption taken
OD-01
Workshop duration
180-minute core simulation plus an optional 25-minute real-company translation extension. The run-of-show is built to that boundary.
OD-02
Second-round capital mechanic
Hybrid: $8.0M base per team plus up to $4.0M earned by the board pitch rubric. Playtests may test fixed and differentiated by changing the base.
OD-03
Payer baselines and names
Names kept from v0.1. CommunityCare starting rating set at 3.5. Memberships, budgets and starting measure values set in this pack.
OD-04
Which measures
Ten measures: M1, M2, M3, M4, M5, M7, M9, M10, M11, M12 from the twelve candidates. Colorectal screening and ED follow-up held in reserve.

Participants see result drivers (which investments moved which leading KPIs and measures, and which multipliers applied). Formulas and parameters live in a facilitator appendix. Full model reveal is offered after the session on request.
OD-06

Normalized scoring: lift divided by headroom, benefit divided by 4-Star bonus value. Archetype-specific board mandates are shown but not scored.
OD-07

Hybrid: a free-form grounded assistant limited to the team's data room, plus four scripted analyst roles (analyst, challenger, explainer, pitch coach). The assistant refuses to produce figures without a cited artifact.

Facilitator scores the rubric; an AI-assisted rubric produces a suggested score the facilitator can accept or override with a note.
OD-09

Per-participant capture, organization-level aggregate, named owners only on the organization's own map, 12-month retention, consent line on the form.

OD-11
Randomness
Deterministic seed in V1 for comparability; variable mode available for replay.
OD-12
Participant prerequisites
No pre-work. A two-page Stars primer is offered as optional pre-read and summarized in the 12-minute briefing. Roles are assigned at the table.
A-13
Measure thresholds
Game constructs chosen to place each payer where the narrative needs it. Not CMS cut points.
A-14
Weight classes
Outcome and intermediate outcome 3, patient experience and complaints/access 2, process 1, as understood for the 2026 Star Ratings. Verify before the pilot.

4-Star bonus value
$600 per member per year illustrative for crossing 4.0 Stars. Used only for the financial dimension.
A-16

An abstraction for implementation bandwidth. Investments carry a demand; payers carry a quarterly supply. Overrun slows progress proportionally.
A-17

Two-year totals in the ranges from v0.1 where given; six non-AI operational cards added so that portfolios can mix technology with capacity, incentives and access.
A-18
Data room size
30 artifacts per payer, 8 domains, each tagged to a root cause, misleading signal or neutral. Brightcone generates the artifact bodies from these specifications; Girish reviews.

2.1 Horizon Health: PE-backed challenger
Fast, modern, thin on history and relationships.
Profile

52,400 (grew from 19,000 in 30 months)

17 counties across Arizona, Texas and Florida; 9 counties entered in the last 18 months
Product mix
88% MA-PD HMO, 12% PPO; one D-SNP launched this year (3,100 members)
Dual-eligible / LIS share
14% dual, 21% LIS
Average member age
71.2 (younger than market)
Starting overall rating
3.0 Stars (simulated)
Revenue
$690M illustrative
Round 1 capital
$18.0M
Round 2 capital
$8.0M base plus up to $4.0M earned at the board pitch
Implementation capacity
10 capacity points per quarter
Data readiness score
3 of 5 (modern stack, 40% of members have under 12 months of history)
Care management staff
22 care managers, 4 pharmacists, 3 outreach coordinators
Contact center
68 agents, 2 vendors, abandonment 11%
Technology
Single cloud data platform (2 years old), one CRM, modern core admin; 4 source systems
Provider network
Mostly fee-for-service contracts in new counties; 2 risk-bearing groups in legacy counties
Population
Younger, faster-growing membership with incomplete longitudinal history. Digital engagement rates look strong but are concentrated among healthy members in legacy counties. New-county members skew older, more rural and less reachable by app or SMS.
Board mandate
Reach 4.0 Stars within two rating cycles to support the next funding round, without slowing county expansion. The board is comfortable with speed and uncomfortable with anything that looks like a two-year foundation program.
Visible advantages
	•	Modern cloud data platform; integrations take weeks, not quarters
	•	Capital is available and the board decides quickly
	•	Leadership appetite for AI is high; no legacy vendor contracts to unwind
	•	Low readmission baseline because the population is younger
Visible constraints
	•	40% of members lack 12 months of claims and pharmacy history, so risk models and gap lists are unstable
	•	No quality incentives in new-county provider contracts; alerts to those providers are ignored
	•	Quality operations team is six people and has never run a full Stars cycle
	•	Complaint rate is already at 2 Stars and rising with growth
Hidden root causes (discoverable in the data room)
ID
Root cause
What is actually happening
H-RC1
History gap mis-targets everything
Members enrolled under 12 months are excluded from or mis-scored in adherence and gap models. Outreach lists over-represent legacy-county members who are already adherent and miss the new-county members driving the shortfall. Any predictive investment inherits this unless identity and history stitching from prior-plan data is funded first.
H-RC2
Digital-first engagement mis-segments
The engagement platform defaults to app and SMS. 61% of new-county members over 75 have never opened the app. Outreach reports show high 'reach' because the message was sent, not because anyone read it. Complaints are rising from members who receive messages intended for other households (shared phone numbers, address churn).
H-RC3
Providers in new counties have no reason to act
New-county contracts are fee-for-service with no quality incentive. Gap alerts go to a portal with 9% open rate. Blood pressure control sits at 2 Stars mainly because readings are not captured, not because members are uncontrolled.

ID
Signal
Why it misleads
H-MS1
App engagement looks excellent
A 44% monthly active rate is real but comes from healthy legacy-county members. Teams that invest in more digital engagement will see leading KPIs rise and measures barely move.
H-MS2
Call volume per member is low
Low volume reads as low demand. It is actually 11% abandonment and a vendor IVR that drops calls after 4 minutes. Complaints and CAHPS customer service are the downstream cost.
Signature AI trap
Automate everything. Speed outpaces validation, governance and organizational readiness. The signature failure is a personalized engagement agent launched on bad identity data that sends inappropriate outreach to thousands of members.

2.2 Heritage Medicare: 15-year legacy incumbent
Scale, history and data everywhere, none of it joined up.
Profile
Members
431,000 across three acquired books (Heritage core 268,000; Lakeshore 97,000; Pinecrest 66,000)
Geography
6 states in the Midwest and Northeast, 140 counties
Product mix
61% HMO, 27% PPO, 12% D-SNP
Dual-eligible / LIS share
29% dual, 38% LIS
Average member age
74.8
Starting overall rating
3.5 Stars (simulated); Lakeshore contract at 3.0
Revenue
$5.9B illustrative
Round 1 capital
$15.0M of a $25M to $27M two-year transformation envelope
Round 2 capital
$8.0M base plus up to $4.0M earned at the board pitch
Implementation capacity
8 capacity points per quarter (large teams, slow change control)
Data readiness score
2 of 5 (14 source systems, 3 CRMs, 11% member identity mismatch across books)
Care management staff
210 care managers, 6 pharmacists, 40 outreach staff
Contact center
540 agents in-house, legacy IVR, abandonment 14%
Technology
On-premise data warehouse plus a partial cloud lake; 14 source systems; 3 CRMs; 2 claims platforms
Provider network
Broad; 35% of members attributed to value-based contracts with quality incentives; rest fee-for-service
Population
Older, more complex, with a high dual-eligible share concentrated in the Lakeshore book. Social barriers (transport, housing instability) and fragmented care across many health systems reduce the lift any single intervention can produce. Longitudinal data is rich for the core book and poor for acquired books.
Board mandate
Hold 4.0 Stars on the core contract and lift Lakeshore to 3.5 within two cycles. The board has approved a $25M transformation envelope and expects visible progress at each quarterly review. A failed flagship program two years ago makes the board allergic to long foundation builds.
Visible advantages
	•	Scale: a one-point adherence lift is worth more here than anywhere
	•	Ten years of clean history on 268,000 core members
	•	Established value-based contracts covering 35% of members
	•	Institutional knowledge of Stars operations; strong quality team of 38
Visible constraints
	•	Three member identity schemes; 11% of members cannot be matched confidently across pharmacy, medical and CRM data
	•	Pharmacy outreach capacity reaches only the top 15% of at-risk members
	•	Discharge (ADT) feeds cover 2 of 5 major health systems; the rest arrive 9 days late via claims
	•	Change control cycle is 6 weeks; any system change competes with a core claims platform migration
Hidden root causes (discoverable in the data room)
ID
Root cause
What is actually happening
L-RC1
Identity mismatch poisons every list
Acquired books use different member IDs and address standards. 11% of members are duplicated or unmatched. Gap lists are wrong for those members, outreach mail goes to old addresses, and predictive models train on fragmented records. The core-book numbers look fine, which hides the problem.
L-RC2
Adherence is capacity-capped, not insight-capped
The pharmacy adherence program is well designed and already targets the right members. Six pharmacists reach 15% of the at-risk list per quarter. A better model adds nothing until outreach capacity or automation of refill reminders is funded.
L-RC3
Readmissions are a data latency problem
Care managers learn of 60% of discharges from claims 9 days later, after the 7-day follow-up window. ADT integration with the three missing health systems would move the readmission measure more than any AI investment.

ID
Signal
Why it misleads
L-MS1
Enterprise adherence looks like 4 Stars
The blended number is 87.5%. Core book is 89.2%, Lakeshore is 82.1%. Teams that read the enterprise view under-invest in adherence and lose the Lakeshore contract rating.
L-MS2
CAHPS looks mid-pack and stable
Customer Service at 87 reads as acceptable. Abandonment is 14% and rising since the IVR vendor contract lapsed. Complaints per 1,000 are at 3 Stars now and will drop a star within a year without action, regardless of AI investment.
Signature AI trap
Modernize everything before value. A $6M data modernization plus $5M identity program consumes all capacity for a year, shows no measure movement at the board pitch, and loses Round 2 capital. The lesson is to sequence foundations and near-term value: identity for Lakeshore only, ADT feeds, pharmacy capacity.

Data readiness score
3 of 5 (excellent clinical data from the system EHR, weak payer-side operations data)
Care management staff

Contact center
95 agents, abandonment 6%
Technology
Shared EHR with the sponsoring system; plan runs a separate core admin and a small warehouse; 6 source systems
Provider network
Sponsoring system employs 70% of attributed PCPs (salaried, no Stars-linked compensation); 30% independent
Population
Geographically concentrated, well attributed, with rich clinical context from the shared EHR. Access is the binding constraint: primary care panels are full and third-next-available appointment is 24 days. Rural edges of the region have transport barriers.
Board mandate
Reach 4.0 Stars and prove the plan-provider model to the system board, which is weighing whether to keep the plan. Capital is modest and every dollar is compared against clinical investments the system could make instead.
Visible advantages
	•	Shared EHR: lab, vitals and encounter data are current and complete for 82% of members
	•	Trusted clinical relationships; care managers sit inside clinics
	•	Low complaint rate and strong customer service scores
	•	Blood pressure and glycemic control already at 4 Stars
Visible constraints
	•	PCP panels are full; outreach generates demand the clinics cannot absorb
	•	Employed clinicians are salaried with no quality compensation; plan quality is not on their scorecard
	•	Clinical recommendations go to a plan portal; clinicians work in the EHR inbox
	•	Plan IT depends on system IT for any EHR-facing change; queue is 2 quarters
Hidden root causes (discoverable in the data room)
ID
Root cause
What is actually happening
C-RC1
Recommendations land where nobody works
The plan's gap and risk recommendations are delivered to a plan portal with a 6% clinician open rate. The sponsoring system's clinicians live in the EHR inbox. Until recommendations are delivered inside the EHR workflow with an agreed owner, insight quality is irrelevant.
C-RC2
Access, not awareness, caps screening and appointments
Breast screening outreach already reaches 78% of eligible members. Third-next-available appointment is 24 days and imaging slots are booked 6 weeks out. Getting Appointments and Care Quickly sits at 2 Stars for the same reason. More outreach raises complaints, not screening.
C-RC3
Affiliation is assumed to mean alignment
Plan and system leadership believe the shared ownership guarantees adoption. Clinicians receive no Stars-linked compensation and see plan requests as administrative burden. Care managers employed by the system report to clinic directors, not the plan.
Misleading surface signals
ID
Signal
Why it misleads
C-MS1
Clinical data completeness is best-in-class
Predictive models validate beautifully on this data. Teams conclude that insight is the gap. It is not; action is.
C-MS2
Customer Service CAHPS at 4 Stars
Members like the people they reach. The problem is the appointment they cannot get, which shows up in a different CAHPS measure and in disenrollment.
Signature AI trap
Assume affiliation guarantees adoption. Value comes from coordinated action (workflow placement, incentives, capacity), not from data possession. The signature failure is a provider copilot that produces excellent recommendations which clinicians ignore.

5. Simulation model parameters
The model is a quarterly state machine. It must be credible enough to support executive reasoning, transparent enough to debug, and simple enough to facilitate. These are the rules the spreadsheet model (WP6a) implements and the engine (WP6b) ports.
Layer
Rule
State per payer
For each measure: current value, leading KPI values; for each investment: status (not funded, building, live, paused, cancelled), progress 0 to 1, adoption 0 to 1; capacity used; identity match rate; cumulative spend; incident log; relationship penalty flags
Quarterly transition
Progress += 1 / duration for each building investment, scaled by min(1, capacity available / capacity demanded). Teams may stagger start quarters within a round (default: all start in Q1); the facilitator console records the schedule. Go-live when progress reaches 1. Leading KPIs respond the quarter after go-live. Measures respond in the following rating year per the lag column in the measure model.
Realized effect
effect = base_effect × payer_eff × prereq_mult × adoption × capacity_mult × diminishing × lag_share. payer_eff: High 1.0, Medium 0.6, Low 0.3. prereq_mult: 1.0 if prerequisites met, 0.5 otherwise, with the specific penalties on each card taking precedence. adoption: 0.15 to 0.30 baseline per card, 0.60 with I11, 0.85 with I11 plus I15, plus 0.10 with I18. capacity_mult as above.
Dependency multipliers
I1 live: ×1.4 on I3, I7, I8 and removes the identity penalty. I2 live: ×1.3 on I5, I6, I8 from the quarter after completion. I14 live: ×2.0 on realized I3 effect. I17 live: ×2.0 on I4. I15 live: unlocks full effect of I5 and I6 at Horizon and CommunityCare.
Diminishing returns
Second investment acting on the same measure realizes 60% of its base effect; third 30%. Effects never exceed the measure's stated headroom.
Decay
As stated per card; applied annually from go-live unless the named control (I12 or maintenance funding) is live.
Crisis logic
After Round 2, the engine evaluates each event's trigger against the team's state and recommends one event and a severity; facilitator confirms or overrides with an audit note. I10 halves probability and lowers severity one level. Severity sets the leading-KPI effects listed per event.
Round 2 capital
Hybrid: $8.0M base plus earned capital from the board pitch rubric. Facilitator may set a different base per team with an audit note.
Uncertainty
Each base effect is a range; the engine draws the midpoint in deterministic mode (seed fixed) or a uniform draw within the range in variable mode. V1 playtests use deterministic mode. Displayed values carry a 'simulated / projected' label and a confidence band of ±20% of effect.
Regression suite
At least 10 portfolios per payer (the two viable strategies, the two failure modes, do-nothing, all-foundation, all-AI, all-operations, max-capacity overrun, and a balanced portfolio) with expected directional outcomes recorded before software build.

8. Human-versus-AI operating model exercise
Process: Quality-gap closure. Teams assign one of four options to each step and answer the six decision dimensions for any step given to an agent.
Step
Activity
Facilitator note
1
Ingest data (claims, pharmacy, labs, ADT, CRM)
Automation is low-risk here; the question is data quality thresholds and who owns exceptions
2
Identify gap (member meets measure denominator, numerator open)
Rules-based; agent may run it; human owns the rules
3
Prioritize member (risk, headroom, reachability, equity)
Predictive model; human sets weights; subgroup monitoring required
4
Select intervention (channel, message, provider route)
Highest-consequence decision for member experience; human review of new templates
5
Initiate outreach (call, SMS, mail, app, pharmacist)
Bounded autonomy possible for low-risk reminders with consent; human for sensitive conditions
6
Notify provider (alert, copilot, incentive report)
Must land in the clinician's workflow; agent may route, human owns the relationship
7
Follow up (second contact, barrier resolution, scheduling)
Agent can schedule; care manager owns barrier conversations
8
Capture evidence (supplemental data, chart retrieval)
Automation with audit trail; human spot check
9
Close gap (numerator event confirmed, member record updated)
Deterministic; audit log mandatory
Assignment options
	•	Human-owned
	•	AI-assisted (human acts, AI recommends)
	•	Agent-executed with approval
	•	Bounded autonomous execution (within thresholds, with monitoring)
Decision dimensions teams must answer
	•	Authority: who may recommend, approve, act, pause, override, shut down
	•	Accountability: executive and operational owner
	•	Controls: validation, confidence threshold, human review, audit log, exception handling
	•	Data: sources, permissions, lineage, quality thresholds, retention
	•	Adoption: whose job changes, incentive conflicts, capacity needed
	•	Monitoring: performance, drift, bias, complaints, safety, realized value
Scoring
Dimension
Question
Scale
Value
Does the design move leading KPIs faster than the status quo?
0 to 3
Control adequacy
Are controls proportionate to consequence and autonomy at each step?
0 to 3
Adoption feasibility
Will the named humans actually do this given incentives and capacity?
0 to 3
Recoverability
Can a wrong action be detected and reversed before member harm?
0 to 3
Autonomy level does not score. A fully human design with no capacity scores low on value; a fully autonomous design at step 4 scores low on control and recoverability.
